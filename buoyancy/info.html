How the Forces Are Calculated

The simulator drops a solid conical frustum (a cylinder whose two end radii can differ) into still water with its surface at \(y = 0\). A rigid-body physics engine (Avian) integrates the motion; every frame the simulation computes and applies the water's forces. Everything below uses water density \(\rho_w = 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), gravity \(g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\), and the body's pose (position, rotation) and velocities from the physics state.

Mass, gravity, and inertia

The body density slider \(\rho_b\) sets the (uniform) density of the solid. The frustum's volume, with end radii \(r_1\) (side 1), \(r_2\) (side 2) and length \(L\), is

$$ V = \frac{\pi L}{3}\left(r_1^2 + r_1 r_2 + r_2^2\right), \qquad m = \rho_b V. $$

The engine derives the mass \(m\), the centre of mass, and the angular inertia tensor from the collision shape at that density, so translation and rotation stay physically consistent as the sliders move. Gravity is then the engine's own \(\mathbf{F}_g = -m g\,\hat{y}\), acting at the centre of mass. Nothing else is applied while the body is fully in the air.

Interior water (fill & slosh)

The shell's cavity can carry water (fill slider, a fraction of the cavity volume \(V_{\text{cav}}\)). Each step the water's distribution is solved from pure geometry: in the body's frame, the free surface is a plane perpendicular to "up", and its level \(\ell\) is found by bisection so the cavity volume below it matches the target,

$$ V_{\text{water}}(\ell) \;=\; f \, V_{\text{cav}}, $$

using the same mesh-clipping and divergence-theorem volume/centroid machinery as the buoyancy methods. The water then joins the body's mass properties as a point mass \(m_w = \rho_w V_{\text{water}}\) at the solved centroid — a quasi-static (instantly re-settling) model, the free-surface approximation used in ship-stability analysis. Because the combined centre of mass now moves with the body's orientation, a load that pools to one side misaligns gravity and buoyancy and the righting torque changes — cargo shift in a can.

The slosh slider is a demonstration knob: it tilts the direction the water settles toward (as if gravity were tilted for the water only) while the water's weight still pulls straight down. Sliding it piles the same mass to one side and the whole system heels in response — proof that the distribution alone, not the amount, changes the balance. The ⟲ button resets the tilt to zero.

Buoyancy — the common principle

All three methods are estimates of the same integral: hydrostatic pressure over the wetted surface. With gauge pressure \(p(y) = -\rho_w\, g\, y\) (zero at the surface), the divergence theorem gives Archimedes' principle,

$$ \mathbf{F}_B \;=\; -\oint_{S_{\text{sub}}} p\,\hat{n}\, dA \;=\; \rho_w\, g\, V_{\text{sub}}\,\hat{y}, $$

acting at the centre of buoyancy \(\mathbf{c}_B\) (the centroid of the displaced volume). Because gravity acts at the centre of mass and buoyancy at \(\mathbf{c}_B\), any misalignment produces a torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = (\mathbf{c}_B - \mathbf{c}_M) \times \mathbf{F}_B\) — this is what rights (or capsizes) the floating body; no explicit torque model is needed. The three methods differ in how they discretise the submerged region.

Method 1 — voxel grid

The body is sampled once (in its own local frame) on a regular 3D grid. The grid density slider \(n\) sets the number of cells along the body's longest dimension, so the cell edge is \(h = \max(2 r_{\max}, L)/n\). Cells whose centre lies inside the frustum are kept. Each frame, every kept cell centre is transformed to world space; a cell contributes an upward force at its own centre \(\mathbf{x}_i\):

$$ \mathbf{F}_i = \rho_w\, g\, k_i\, h^3\, \hat{y}, \qquad k_i = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{-y_i}{h} + \tfrac{1}{2},\; 0,\; 1\right), $$

where \(k_i\) is the cell's fractional submersion: 1 fully underwater, 0 fully dry, and a linear ramp while the cell straddles the waterline (this is the standard fix for force "popping" as cells cross the surface). Because each force is applied at the cell's own position, the distribution of submerged cells automatically produces the correct net torque about the centre of mass.

Viscous drag (add-on), per cell, resists the velocity of the body at that cell \(\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}_M)\):

$$ \mathbf{F}_{d,i} = -3\, C_d\, \rho_w\, k_i h^3\, \mathbf{v}_i, $$

with \(C_d\) the drag-coefficient slider. Since offset drag forces also produce torque, rotation is damped for free.

Method 2 — surface pressure integration

The hull surface is tessellated into triangles — the hull resolution slider sets the radial segment count (rings along the wall scale with it). Each frame every triangle is transformed to world space and clipped against the water plane, keeping only the submerged part. For each submerged (sub-)triangle with area \(A\), outward unit normal \(\hat{n}\), and centroid at depth \(d\), the hydrostatic pressure force is

$$ \mathbf{F} = -\rho_w\, g\, d\, A\, n_y\, \hat{y}, $$

applied at the centroid. Two deliberate details: no "cap" polygon is needed at the waterline because gauge pressure is zero there, and only the vertical component is kept — the horizontal components cancel exactly over a closed surface, and dropping them removes numerical drift (the resulting torque matches the exact centre-of-buoyancy torque to a fraction of a percent).

Viscous drag (add-on)

Skin friction on the tangential flow, using the ITTC-1957 friction line. Once per frame the Reynolds number and friction coefficient are computed from the body speed \(v\) and length \(L\) (water's kinematic viscosity \(\nu = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\)):

$$ \mathrm{Rn} = \frac{v L}{\nu}, \qquad C_F = \frac{0.075}{(\log_{10} \mathrm{Rn} - 2)^2}, $$

then per submerged triangle, with \(\mathbf{v}_t\) the component of the local point velocity tangent to the face:

$$ \mathbf{F}_{\text{visc}} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\, \rho_w\, C_F\, A\, |\mathbf{v}_t|\, \mathbf{v}_t \cdot C_d. $$

Pressure drag (add-on)

Quadratic drag on the flow normal to each face — the force that realistically damps bobbing and gives a moving hull "grip". With \(v_n = \mathbf{v}_p \cdot \hat{n}\) the normal speed of the face and \(C_{pd}\) the slider:

$$ \mathbf{F}_{\text{pd}} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\, \rho_w\, A\, v_n\, |v_n|\, \hat{n} \cdot C_{pd}. $$

The sign is symmetric: a face pushing into the water is resisted, a retreating face is sucked back — both fall out of the \(v_n |v_n|\) form.

Slamming (add-on)

A water-entry impact force (after Kerner's boat model). For each original hull triangle, the swept-volume rate is tracked between frames, \(\dot{V} = A_{\text{sub}}\, |\mathbf{v}_p|\), and its rate of change — the "violence" of the entry — is

$$ \Gamma = \frac{|\dot{V}(t) - \dot{V}(t - \Delta t)|}{A\, \Delta t}. $$

Triangles moving into the water (\(\cos\theta = \hat{v}_p \cdot \hat{n} > 0\)) receive a stopping force scaled by how violent the entry is, saturating at the \(\Gamma_{\max}\) slider:

$$ \mathbf{F}_{\text{slam}} = -\left(\operatorname{clamp}\!\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma_{\max}}\right)^{\!2} \cos\theta\; m\,|\mathbf{v}_p|\, \frac{2A}{S_{\text{total}}}\; \hat{v}_p, $$

where \(S_{\text{total}}\) is the whole hull's area. The \(m |\mathbf{v}_p|\, 2A / S_{\text{total}}\) cap means the add-on can stop the body but never launch it.

Method 3 — clipped volume (exact)

The same clipped triangle list, but instead of per-face forces, the exact submerged volume and centre of buoyancy are computed with the divergence theorem. For each submerged triangle (centroid \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\), normal \(\hat{n}\), area \(A\)):

$$ V_{\text{sub}} = \sum \bar{y}\, n_y\, A, \qquad \mathbf{c}_B = \frac{1}{V_{\text{sub}}} \sum \frac{\overline{x^2}}{2}\, n_x A \;\hat{x} + \cdots $$

(the second moments use the exact mean of \(x^2\) over a triangle, \(\overline{x^2} = \tfrac{1}{6}(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_1 x_3 + x_2 x_3)\), and likewise for \(y\), \(z\)). The waterline cap again contributes nothing because \(y = 0\) there. A single force \(\mathbf{F}_B = \rho_w g V_{\text{sub}}\,\hat{y}\) is applied at \(\mathbf{c}_B\) — for hydrostatics this reproduces the distributed pressure force and torque exactly, with zero sampling noise. It is the reference the other two methods converge toward as their sliders increase.

Viscous drag (add-on) has no per-face sites here, so it acts at \(\mathbf{c}_B\) as a quadratic + linear pair with a separate rotational damping term (the Jolt Physics pattern):

$$ \mathbf{F}_d = -\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_w \cdot 0.5 \cdot V_{\text{sub}}^{2/3} |\mathbf{v}| \;+\; 1.5\,\rho_w V_{\text{sub}}\right) \mathbf{v}\, C_d, \qquad \boldsymbol{\tau}_d = -0.02\, \rho_w V_{\text{sub}} L^2\, C_d\, \boldsymbol{\omega}, $$

where \(V_{\text{sub}}^{2/3}\) stands in for the projected area of the wetted region.

Waves

The Waves menu sends a single sinusoidal wave train across the surface along \(+x\), so the water level becomes a function of position and time:

$$ \eta(x, t) \;=\; A \sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{c}, $$

with \(A\) the amplitude slider (0 = flat water, the default), \(T\) the period, and \(c\) the speed — so the wavelength is \(\lambda = cT\). The model stays hydrostatic under the instantaneous surface: pressure is still \(\rho_w g \times\) depth, but depth is now measured to \(\eta\) straight above the point. (Real waves also carry orbital velocities and dynamic pressure that decay with depth; like most game water, those are neglected.) Each method adapts differently:

  • Voxel grid — each cell's fractional submersion compares the cell centre against the local surface height \(\eta(x_i, t)\) instead of a constant level.
  • Surface pressure — triangles are clipped by per-vertex signed distances \(d_i = y_i - \eta(x_i, t)\) (the surface is linearised over each edge, the same order of error as the tessellation), and each sub-triangle's pressure depth is taken to the surface above its centroid.
  • Clipped volume — each vertex is sheared vertically by the local wave elevation, \(y' = y - \eta(x, t)\), which maps the wave surface back to a flat plane so the exact plane-clip machinery applies unchanged. The shear has unit Jacobian (it only slides vertical columns), so the volume computed in sheared space is the submerged volume; the centre of buoyancy is un-sheared at its own \(x\) afterwards.

As on flat water, only the vertical pressure component is applied. Under waves the horizontal components no longer cancel exactly (real waves impart drift forces), but that residue is of the same order as the dynamic-pressure terms the hydrostatic model already leaves out — dropping it keeps the three methods comparable and drift-free. A body floating in waves still pitches and heaves realistically, because the distribution of vertical force along the hull follows the passing crests and troughs.
